Short answer: Kanazawa in winter is wet more than it is frozen. Expect cold rain, some days of heavy, wet snow, and slushy streets, so the most important thing you pack is a pair of waterproof shoes with good grip. In return you get the city at its most atmospheric, with rope-wrapped pine trees, snow on the old roofs and crab on every menu.

I teach English just outside Kanazawa, and winter is the season visitors ask me about most. Here is what actually changes between November and March, and how to prepare for it.

## What is the weather like in Kanazawa in winter?

Kanazawa sits on the Sea of Japan side of the country, and in winter cold air crosses that sea and drops its moisture on the coast. That is why the city has its reputation for rain and snow, while Tokyo on the other side of the mountains often has clear, dry winter days.

A few things to expect:

- **Rain is the default.** Many winter days here are grey and drizzly rather than snowy. Locals have an old saying for it: *bentō wasuretemo, kasa wasureruna*, "forget your lunch if you must, but never forget your umbrella."
- **Snow comes in waves.** Some winters bring several heavy snowfalls, others only a few light ones. It changes a lot from year to year, so I won't promise you snow on any particular date.
- **The snow is heavy and wet.** It is not the dry powder of the ski resorts in Hokkaido or Nagano. It melts quickly into slush, which is what soaks your shoes.
- **It is cold, but not extreme.** The bigger problem is the damp. A wet coat in a cold wind feels much colder than the number on the weather app.

Check the forecast in the days before you go. If heavy snow is coming, trains and buses can be delayed, and that matters most if you are planning a day trip.

## What should I wear in Kanazawa in winter?

Think "waterproof" first and "warm" second.

- **Shoes:** The single most useful thing you can bring. Waterproof shoes or boots with a grippy sole. Canvas sneakers will be soaked within an hour on a slushy day. Stone paving, wooden thresholds and the slopes around the old districts can be slippery.
- **Coat:** A warm coat that can handle rain, or a warm layer under a waterproof shell. A hood helps.
- **Layers:** Trains, shops, museums and restaurants are heated, sometimes very well. Layers you can take off inside are more comfortable than one huge sweater.
- **Umbrella:** Bring a compact one, or buy a cheap clear plastic umbrella at any convenience store when you arrive. Many shops and restaurants have an umbrella stand or plastic sleeves at the entrance for wet umbrellas. Use them.
- **Socks:** Pack a spare pair. You may be taking your shoes off at a restaurant or a temple, and wet socks are no fun.

## What are the sprinklers in the middle of the road?

This surprises almost every visitor. On many main streets in Kanazawa, there are small nozzles set into the road surface. When it snows, they spray water to melt the snow before it builds up. They are called *shōsetsu sōchi* (消雪装置), snow-melting sprinklers.

They work well for cars, but for pedestrians they mean one thing: puddles. The water spreads across the road and splashes onto crossings and the edges of sidewalks. The roads department even warns that people crossing the road may get their feet wet when the system is tested before winter.

So when you cross a street on a snowy day, look down, step around the spray, and be glad you packed those waterproof shoes. Many of the smaller lanes don't have sprinklers, which means they keep their snow and can be icy.

## What is yukitsuri at Kenrokuen?

If you visit in winter, you will see pine trees with ropes fanning down from a central pole, like the frame of a tent or an umbrella. This is *yukitsuri* (雪吊り), "snow hanging," and it is the classic winter image of Kanazawa.

The ropes are not decoration. Kanazawa's snow is heavy and wet, and its weight can snap the branches of old pine trees. The ropes take some of that weight and protect the branches.

At Kenrokuen garden, the work usually starts on November 1, beginning with the famous Karasaki pine by the pond, and continues until around mid-December. According to the prefecture's announcement for the 2025 season, it took around 500 workers and about 4,000 kg of straw rope. The ropes come down again in mid-March.

That means the ropes are up for most of the winter, even on days without snow. If you get a day with fresh snow on top of them, you are seeing Kenrokuen at its most photographed.

## Do opening hours change in winter?

Yes, at Kenrokuen. From October 16 to the end of February, the garden's regular hours are 8:00 to 17:00, shorter than the summer hours. Free early entry through some of the gates also starts later in winter, around 6:00, and you have to leave before the paid hours begin. Check the official Kenrokuen page for the current gates and times. I go through the timing in more detail in my [first-timer's itinerary](/articles/kanazawa-first-time-itinerary/).

The other thing that changes is the daylight. Winter afternoons are short here, so plan your outdoor sightseeing for the morning and early afternoon, and keep museums, markets and dinner for later.

Kenrokuen and Kanazawa Castle Park also have a winter evening light-up, usually on selected days in February. Listings for February 2027 show February 6 to 10 and the Saturdays after that, from 18:00, with free entry in the evening (unverified, as I could only find this on an event listing site so far). Check the prefecture's or the tourist association's official pages before you plan around it.

## Is winter a good time to visit Kanazawa?

I think so, as long as you come prepared. Here is the honest trade-off.

**What is better in winter:**

- **The food.** Snow crab season opens in early November, and the female *kobako-gani* is only around until about the end of December. Oden, a hot simmered dish Kanazawa is known for, makes complete sense on a cold night. See my [guide to crab season](/articles/kanazawa-crab-season/) for what to look for.
- **The scenery.** Yukitsuri at Kenrokuen, snow on the roofs of the teahouse district, steam rising from bowls of soup in small restaurants.
- **The evenings.** Kanazawa's izakaya and bars are at their cosiest when it is wet outside. My [guide to the nightlife areas](/articles/kanazawa-nightlife-areas/) explains where to go.

**What is harder in winter:**

- **Wet feet and cold hands** if you don't pack for it.
- **Delays on snowy days**, especially on buses and on day trips into the mountains.
- **Short days**, so fewer hours of daylight for walking around.

If you are planning a day trip to the snowy villages of Shirakawa-go, book the bus early. Winter is the most popular season there. My [Shirakawa-go day trip guide](/articles/shirakawa-go-day-trip/) explains how.

## Say it in Japanese: winter phrases

A few short phrases that come up a lot in winter. You will hear the first two from locals, so it helps to know what they mean.

| Japanese | How to say it | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 寒いですね | *Samui desu ne* (sah-moo-ee dess neh) | "It's cold, isn't it?" The standard winter greeting. Just reply *Sō desu ne* (soh dess neh), "It is." |
| 足元に気をつけて | *Ashimoto ni ki o tsukete* (ah-shee-moh-toh nee kee oh tskeh-teh) | "Watch your step." Staff often say this when you leave on a snowy day. |
| 傘はどこで買えますか？ | *Kasa wa doko de kaemasu ka?* (kah-sah wah doh-koh deh kah-eh-mass kah) | "Where can I buy an umbrella?" |
| 靴を脱ぎますか？ | *Kutsu o nugimasu ka?* (koo-tsoo oh noo-gee-mass kah) | "Should I take off my shoes?" Useful at restaurants with raised floors. |

For more phrases you can use when you get inside and out of the cold, see [how to order at an izakaya](/articles/izakaya-english-kanazawa/).

## A short winter checklist

- Waterproof shoes with grip
- Warm coat with a hood, plus layers
- Compact umbrella
- A spare pair of socks
- Check the weather and transport the night before any day trip
- Outdoor sightseeing in the morning, food and museums later

Winter is when Kanazawa feels most like itself. Come with dry feet and an appetite, and you will have a very good trip.
