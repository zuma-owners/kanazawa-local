**Short answer:** Three words carry most of a trip: *sumimasen* (excuse me), *onegaishimasu* (please, when you ask for something) and *arigatou gozaimasu* (thank you). Add a place name or "this one" in front of *onegaishimasu*, and you can order food, pay, and tell a taxi driver where to go. The rest of this page gives you the handful of extra phrases that come up in restaurants, shops and taxis, and the one big shopping rule that changed in November 2026.

I'm an English coach in Kanazawa, so I spend my days helping Japanese people speak English. Here it's the other way around. You don't need grammar for any of this. You need short phrases, said clearly, and a phone screen to point at when words run out.

*If you're going to an izakaya, I have a separate guide just for that: [How to Order at an Izakaya](/articles/izakaya-english-kanazawa/). This page covers everything else.*

## Which three words should I learn first?

> **Sumimasen.** (soo-mee-mah-sen) — "Excuse me." Also "sorry" and, in a small way, "thank you for the trouble." Use it to get attention, to squeeze past someone, or to start any question.

> **Onegaishimasu.** (oh-neh-guy-shee-mass) — "Please," when you are asking for something. "*Kore,* onegaishimasu" means "This one, please."

> **Arigatou gozaimasu.** (ah-ree-gah-toh go-zai-mass) — "Thank you very much." The polite version. Use it with staff and drivers.

If you remember nothing else, *sumimasen* plus pointing plus *onegaishimasu* will get you surprisingly far.

## How do I get a table at a restaurant or café?

Staff will usually greet you with *irasshaimase* (welcome). You don't need to answer it; a nod is fine. Then they will ask how many people. Hold up fingers or say:

> **Hitori desu / Futari desu.** (hee-toh-ree dess / foo-tah-ree dess) — "One person / Two people."

At cafés and casual places you may also be asked whether you're eating there or taking it away:

> **Tennai de.** (ten-nai deh) — "To eat here."
> **Mochikaeri de.** (moh-chee-kah-eh-ree deh) — "To take away."

Some noodle, curry and set-meal restaurants use a **ticket machine** (*shokken-ki*) by the door. You pay at the machine first, take the ticket, and hand it to the staff. If the buttons are all in Japanese, it is completely fine to ask for help:

> **Tsukaikata o oshiete kudasai.** (tsoo-kai-kah-tah oh oh-shee-eh-teh koo-dah-sigh) — "Could you show me how to use it?"

## How do I order and ask about food?

Point at the menu or a photo and say *kore, onegaishimasu*. To ask what the place is good at, *osusume wa nan desu ka?* (oh-soo-soo-meh wah nahn dess kah, "What do you recommend?") works everywhere, not just in izakaya.

If you have an allergy, say it early and show it in writing too. Spoken Japanese plus a note on your phone is much safer than either one alone.

> **[Item] arerugii ga arimasu.** (ah-reh-roo-gee gah ah-ree-mass) — "I have a [item] allergy."

Useful words to put in front: *ebi* (shrimp), *kani* (crab), *kurumi* (walnut), *piinattsu* (peanut), *komugi* (wheat), *tamago* (egg). In Kanazawa, with so much seafood, the crab and shrimp ones are worth knowing. For a serious allergy, please also carry a written card in Japanese; a short phrase is not a guarantee.

If you're vegetarian, the honest advice is that Japanese stocks often use fish (*dashi*), even in dishes that look like vegetables only. Asking *niku to sakana wa haitte imasu ka?* (nee-koo toh sah-kah-nah wah hait-teh ee-mass kah, "Does it contain meat or fish?") is a good start, but expect that some answers will be "a little, in the broth."

## How do I pay?

> **Okaikei onegaishimasu.** (oh-kai-kay oh-neh-guy-shee-mass) — "The bill, please."

> **Kaado de ii desu ka?** (kah-doh deh ee dess kah) — "Is card OK?"

You can replace *kaado* with *Suica* or *ICOCA* if you use an IC card, or just hold up the card and look hopeful. Many places take cards now, but small, older shops can still be cash only, so carry some yen.

Two things that confuse visitors:

- **There is no tipping.** Not at restaurants, not in taxis, not at hotels.
- **Change goes on a little tray.** At many registers you place your cash or card on a small tray instead of handing it over. The staff will put your change there too.

## What do I say in shops?

> **Kore wa ikura desu ka?** (koh-reh wah ee-koo-rah dess kah) — "How much is this?"

> **Shichaku shite mo ii desu ka?** (shee-chah-koo shee-teh moh ee dess kah) — "Can I try this on?"

> **Fukuro wa iranai desu.** (foo-koo-roh wah ee-rah-nai dess) — "I don't need a bag."

That last one is handy. Since July 2020, shops in Japan have charged for plastic bags, so at a convenience store or supermarket the cashier will often ask *fukuro wa irimasu ka?* ("Do you need a bag?"). Say *iranai desu* (no thanks) or *onegaishimasu* (yes, please). In gift shops near Higashi Chaya or Omicho Market, staff may offer a paper bag for a souvenir; the same two answers work.

## Has tax-free shopping changed?

Yes, and this is the most important update on this page. According to the Japan Tourism Agency, **from purchases made on November 1, 2026, tax-free shopping for visitors works as a refund**, not as a discount at the shop.

What that means in practice:

1. At a tax-free shop you pay the **full price, including consumption tax**, and show your passport.
2. When you **leave Japan** at the airport or seaport, customs checks that you are taking the items out of the country, using the tax-free terminals there.
3. After that check, the tax amount is refunded. You need to leave Japan within **90 days** of the purchase.

Food, drinks and cosmetics that you use up while still in Japan are not refunded, and the old sealed "do not open" bags for consumables are gone. So if you buy sake or sweets in Kanazawa and drink or eat them at your hotel, there is no refund for those.

Refund methods and timing are handled by each shop or its refund partner, so ask at the counter:

> **Menzei dekimasu ka?** (men-zay deh-kee-mass kah) — "Can I buy this tax-free?"

> **Henkin wa dou narimasu ka?** (hen-keen wah doh nah-ree-mass kah) — "How does the refund work?"

Japanese-language information on this change is far more detailed than the English, and some older English blog posts still describe the old system. If in doubt, trust the Japan Tourism Agency's tax-free site (linked below) over a travel blog.

## How do I take a taxi?

The easiest place to find a taxi is the taxi stand at **Kanazawa Station**. Downtown you can also wave one down on the main roads. Two habits to know:

- **The rear door opens and closes by itself.** The driver controls it. Don't pull it shut.
- **Show the address, not just the name.** A name in English may not match what the driver knows. The Japanese name or the address on your phone's map is best.

> **[Place] made onegaishimasu.** (mah-deh oh-neh-guy-shee-mass) — "To [place], please." For example, *Kenrokuen made onegaishimasu.*

> **Koko de daijoubu desu.** (koh-koh deh die-joe-boo dess) — "Here is fine." Use it when you want to get out a little before the address.

> **Kaado de ii desu ka?** — "Is card OK?" Ask before you set off if you have no cash.

Taxi apps work in Kanazawa too. Uber, for example, dispatches regular local taxis here (it started in the city in July 2023), and Japanese taxi apps are also used. With an app, the destination is already typed in, which removes most of the stress.

For a lot of trips, though, the bus is cheaper and easy once you know how it works. My [bus guide](/articles/kanazawa-bus-guide/) explains the Loop Bus and how to pay.

## A tip on pronunciation

Japanese vowels are short and even: *a* as in "father," *i* as in "see," *u* as in "food," *e* as in "get," *o* as in "go." The final *u* in *desu* and *-masu* is almost silent, which is why I write "dess" and "mass." Say every syllable at the same speed and you will be understood, even if your accent is strong. In my lessons, the people who get understood are not the ones with perfect pronunciation. They are the ones who speak clearly and don't apologize first.
