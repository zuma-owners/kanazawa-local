**Short answer:** The Higashi Chaya district is a small, free-to-walk street of wooden teahouses about five minutes on foot from the **Hashibacho** (橋場町) bus stop. Plan on about an hour. If you want to see inside a real teahouse, two of them open to visitors during the day: **Shima** (¥500 for adults) and **Kaikaro** (¥750 for adults). Both close in the late afternoon, so go in the daytime if the interiors matter to you.

Higashi Chaya is the picture on most Kanazawa posters: a narrow stone street, two-storey wooden fronts with fine lattices, and almost no modern signs. It is beautiful, and it is also small, busy and a place where people still live and work. This guide covers what to see, how to time it and how to be a good guest there.

*Information checked on the Kanazawa City Tourism Association's official site, the teahouses' official pages and the Agency for Cultural Affairs' Japanese-language listing, on November 24, 2026. Hours and prices change, so check before you go.*

## What is Higashi Chaya?

A *chaya* (茶屋) here is not a café. It is a teahouse where geiko, as geisha are called in Kanazawa, entertain guests with music, dance and conversation. In 1820, the city's teahouses were moved into a few set districts away from the centre, and Higashi ("east") Chaya was one of them. The teahouse Shima dates from that same year.

Kanazawa has three geisha districts: **Higashi Chaya**, **Nishi Chaya** and **Kazuemachi**. Higashi is the largest and the most visited.

The national listing for the district, which I found on the Agency for Cultural Affairs' Japanese site, adds some detail that is hard to find in English. The area was selected as a national **Important Preservation District for Groups of Traditional Buildings** (重要伝統的建造物群保存地区, *jūyō dentōteki kenzōbutsugun hozon chiku*) on **November 14, 2001**. It covers only about **1.8 hectares**, and its teahouse buildings date from the late Edo period to the early Meiji period. In other words, it is a real old neighbourhood, not a reconstruction, and it is small enough to walk end to end in a few minutes.

## How do I get to Higashi Chaya?

- **By bus:** get off at **Hashibacho** (橋場町). The Kanazawa Loop Bus, Hokutetsu local buses and West JR Buses all stop there, and the district is about 5 minutes' walk. The right loop of the Loop Bus reaches it first from the station. My [Kanazawa bus guide](/articles/kanazawa-bus-guide/) explains which stand to use and how to pay.
- **By the Flat Bus:** the small Zaimoku-route Flat Bus stops at **Asanogawa Ohashi** (浅野川大橋), also about 5 minutes away.
- **By taxi:** about 10 minutes from Kanazawa Station.

From the bus stop you cross the **Asano River**. The riverside path is a pleasant extra walk before or after the teahouse streets.

## What should I see there?

The streets themselves are the main thing, and they cost nothing. Walk the main street, then the side lanes. Look up at the second floors: the teahouses were built with their guest rooms upstairs.

To see inside, choose one of the two teahouses that open to the public:

| Teahouse | What it is | Hours | Admission |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shima** (志摩) | Built in 1820 with the district; a national Important Cultural Property | 9:30–17:30 (Mar–Nov), 9:30–17:00 (Dec–Feb), open every day | Adults ¥500, 16 and under ¥300 |
| **Kaikaro** (懐華楼) | A teahouse dating from 1820, designated a Kanazawa City preserved building | 10:00–17:00, irregular closing days | Adults ¥750, ages 6–18 ¥500, under 6 free |

Both offer green tea with a Japanese sweet for an extra charge. The official pages I checked did not list that price, so ask at the entrance. The tourism association suggests about 15 minutes for Shima. Kaikaro publishes a calendar of closing days on its site, so check it before you go.

If you only have time for one: Shima is the one with national cultural-property status and is open every day, while Kaikaro has shorter hours and also runs events with geiko (more below). Either way, you will understand the street much better after you have stood in an upstairs guest room.

## Can I see geisha at Higashi Chaya?

Not in the way some visitors expect. The teahouses' evening entertainment is mostly for regular guests, usually by introduction, so you cannot just walk in at night and book a geisha.

There are a few doors open to visitors, though:

- The **Kanazawa City Tourism Association** holds geisha performances for the public in the three districts on **designated Saturdays**. Dates are announced on its official site, Visit Kanazawa, so check there while you plan.
- **Kaikaro** lists geisha performances and events that the general public can join. Details and dates are on its official site.

If you do see a geiko on the street, she is most likely on her way to work. Give her space, and don't block her way for a photo.

## When is the best time to go?

The streets are public, so you can walk them at any hour. A few things to plan around:

- **For the teahouse interiors,** go between about 10:00 and 16:30, when both Shima and Kaikaro are open.
- **For fewer people,** go early. The tourism association lists early-morning sightseeing as one of the district's features, and the street is much easier to photograph before the day crowds arrive. It tends to be busiest in the middle of the day, so late afternoon is another option.
- **In the evening,** the public teahouses are closed, so think of it as a short walk after dinner rather than a sightseeing stop.
- **In winter,** Shima closes at 17:00, and it gets dark early. Wear shoes with grip if there is snow (see my [winter guide](/articles/kanazawa-winter-guide/)).

## Etiquette: how to be a good guest

Higashi Chaya is a working neighbourhood, so the basics matter more here than in most places:

- **Don't eat while walking.** Kanazawa's tourism association says it plainly: in Japan, eating while you walk is generally seen as bad manners. If you buy a snack, ask the shop where you can eat it.
- **Ask before you photograph** inside shops, and be careful with people's privacy. Many buildings are private homes or businesses, so don't open doors or step inside entrances that aren't shops.
- **Take your rubbish with you.** There are very few public bins in Japan.
- **Keep the street clear.** The lanes are narrow, so step to the side when you stop for photos.

## Before you go

- Bus to **Hashibacho**, then walk about 5 minutes.
- The teahouse pages I checked did not say which payments they take, so carry some cash just in case.
- Go in the daytime for the teahouse interiors, early for fewer crowds.
- No eating while walking, and ask before photos.
