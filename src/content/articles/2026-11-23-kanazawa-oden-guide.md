**Short answer:** Oden is a pot of ingredients slowly simmered in a light dashi broth, and in Kanazawa it is everyday food, eaten all year, not only in winter. What makes the Kanazawa version different is a handful of local ingredients: **kuruma-fu** (a big ring of wheat gluten that soaks up the broth), **akamaki** (a red-and-white swirled fish cake), **bai-gai** (a sea snail you pull out with a toothpick) and, in early winter, **kani-men**, a whole female crab shell packed with meat and roe. You usually order one or two pieces at a time and keep going until you are full.

I'm ZUMA, an English coach who lives next door to Kanazawa. This page doesn't recommend specific oden restaurants. It's here so that when you sit down at one, you know what's in the pot, what to point at, and what to say.

## What is oden, exactly?

Oden is found all over Japan, from convenience store counters to old restaurants. The basic idea is always the same: daikon radish, eggs, fish cakes, tofu and other ingredients sit in a warm broth for hours and slowly take on its flavor. You pick what you want, and it comes to you in a shallow bowl with a little of the broth.

In Kanazawa, according to the city's official tourism site, the broth is gentle and savory, built on kombu (kelp) and dried fish flakes. Every restaurant makes its own, so the taste changes from place to place. Some use local seasonings, such as soy sauce from Ono, a district of Kanazawa, and salt from the Noto Peninsula.

If you have eaten oden elsewhere in Japan, the broth may feel lighter in color than you expect. The difference is in the ingredients.

## Which Kanazawa ingredients should I try?

These are the ones that you won't often see in oden outside the Hokuriku region. Most oden restaurants have a written menu or a board, sometimes only in Japanese, so the kanji are here too.

**Kuruma-fu (車麩, koo-roo-mah-foo).** A large, round cake of wheat gluten with a hole in the middle, a bit like a flat doughnut. On its own it is light and dry. After a long time in the pot, it becomes soft and full of broth. If you only try one local thing, try this.

**Akamaki (赤巻, ah-kah-mah-kee).** A fish cake made by laying red fish paste over white, rolling it up and steaming it, so each slice has a red-and-white swirl. It is springy and slightly chewy. You'll also see it in New Year dishes in this area.

**Fukashi (ふかし, foo-kah-shee).** A flat steamed fish cake, usually white or pink. It's softer than the firm kamaboko you may know, but springier than the fluffy hanpen in Tokyo-style oden.

**Bai-gai (バイ貝 or ばい貝, bye-guy).** A sea snail from the Sea of Japan, served in its shell. The meat is firm, a little crunchy and slightly sweet. You pull it out with a toothpick, twisting as you go so the whole thing comes out. Some people also drink the broth left inside the shell.

**Gensuke daikon (源助大根, gen-skeh dai-kon).** Daikon is in every oden pot in Japan, but Kanazawa has its own variety, one of the official Kaga vegetables: short, thick and naturally sweet. It is shipped from about mid-October to February, so this is the season for it. Not every place will say which daikon they use, but if you see 源助 on the menu, that's the one.

**Kanazawa shungiku (春菊, shun-gee-koo).** Another Kaga vegetable, a mild, tender type of chrysanthemum greens. It is added near the end rather than simmered for hours. In season from about October to April.

The usual oden ingredients are there too: *daikon*, *tamago* (egg), *ganmo* (fried tofu with vegetables), *konnyaku* (a firm jelly made from konjac), *atsuage* (thick fried tofu) and more. Every pot is a little different.

## What is kani-men, and when can I get it?

**Kani-men (かに面, kah-nee men)** is the big one. It is a shell of *kobako-gani*, the female snow crab, packed with its meat and both kinds of roe (the orange roe inside and the small eggs carried under the body), then warmed in the oden broth. It takes a lot of work, so restaurants make a limited number.

Because it uses kobako-gani, it is only around during that crab's short fishing season. In Ishikawa the kobako-gani season runs from **November 6 to December 29**, so kani-men is mainly a November and December thing. Availability and how it's made differ by restaurant, so it's worth asking or checking before you go. Kani-men is also usually one of the most expensive things on an oden menu, and the price is often written as 時価 (*jika*, market price). It is fine to ask before you order.

## How does ordering oden work?

At most oden places you sit at a counter facing the pot, or at a table. A few things to know:

- **You order by the piece.** Start with two or three items, then add more as you go. There's no need to order everything at once, and nobody expects you to.
- **Prices are often per piece.** Some menus list each item with a price, some only show it on a board, and seasonal items may say *jika*. If you can't find a price, ask (phrase below).
- **Mustard is on the side.** A small pot of *karashi* (Japanese mustard) is common. It is hot, so use a little.
- **Drinks come first.** Staff will often ask what you want to drink before anything else. Oden goes well with beer, sake or a cup of green tea. If you'd like help with sake, see my [izakaya ordering guide](/articles/izakaya-english-kanazawa/).
- **Some places end early.** The official tourism site notes that some oden restaurants close when their ingredients sell out, and popular ones can have a line on weekends. Going a little earlier in the evening helps.
- **Rice at the end.** Some older oden restaurants also serve *chameshi*, a lightly seasoned rice, which is a nice way to finish.

If the counter is busy and in Japanese, pointing is completely normal. Point at the pot, or at the word on the menu, and say *kore, onegaishimasu*.

## Say it in Japanese

> **Osusume wa nan desu ka?** (oh-soo-soo-meh wah nahn dess kah) — "What do you recommend?" Good for your first round.

> **Kore to kore, onegaishimasu.** (koh-reh toh koh-reh, oh-neh-guy-shee-mass) — "This and this, please." Point as you say each *kore*.

> **Kani-men wa arimasu ka?** (kah-nee men wah ah-ree-mass kah) — "Do you have kani-men?" Worth asking in November and December.

> **Kore wa ikura desu ka?** (koh-reh wah ee-koo-rah dess kah) — "How much is this?" Useful for anything marked 時価.

## Is oden only a winter food?

No. Kanazawa's own tourism site describes oden as something people here eat all through the year. Winter is still the best time to go, though, because that is when kani-men and Gensuke daikon are in season, and a hot bowl of broth makes a lot of sense on a cold, wet Kanazawa night.
